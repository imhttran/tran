# Commercial Loan Servicing Manual

## Escrow Administration

Escrow accounts are established to collect and hold funds for property taxes, hazard insurance premiums, and other recurring obligations identified in the loan agreement. Each escrow account is analyzed at least once every twelve months to determine whether the collected amounts are sufficient to cover the anticipated disbursements for the coming escrow period. The analysis compares the projected annual disbursements, the lowest projected balance, and the permitted cushion.

A cushion is the amount the servicer may require the borrower to hold above the anticipated disbursements. The permitted cushion is generally one-sixth of the total annual escrow disbursements, subject to the terms of the loan agreement and applicable requirements. A shortage exists when the projected balance falls below the required target during the escrow period. When an analysis projects a shortage, the servicer may allow the borrower to repay the shortage in equal monthly installments over at least twelve months, or in a shorter period if the borrower requests it, unless the loan agreement provides otherwise.

A surplus exists when the projected balance exceeds the target. If the surplus is small, the servicer may credit it against future escrow payments; if it exceeds the permitted threshold, the servicer must refund the surplus to the borrower within the period required by applicable rules. A shortage or a surplus is not a delinquency: it reflects a timing difference between collections and disbursements rather than a missed payment.

The annual escrow account statement summarizes the account activity for the prior computation year, the projected activity for the coming year, the balance at the lowest point, and the reason for any change in the monthly escrow payment. When the monthly escrow payment changes, the servicer notifies the borrower of the new amount and the effective date before the change takes effect.

Disbursements are made when the obligation is due, provided sufficient funds are available in the account. If funds are insufficient at the time of a disbursement, the servicer may advance its own funds to protect the lien and later seek reimbursement from the borrower. Advances for taxes and insurance are recoverable even when the escrow account has no balance, because unpaid taxes or lapsed insurance can impair the collateral.

## Insurance Requirements

The borrower must maintain hazard insurance on the property in an amount at least equal to the lesser of the outstanding principal balance or the maximum amount available under the loan agreement. The servicer verifies that coverage remains in force by reviewing evidence of insurance, which may be provided by the borrower, the insurance agent, or an insurance verification service. Evidence of insurance must identify the property, the named insured, the policy effective dates, and the coverage amount.

If the servicer cannot verify coverage, it sends a notice describing the missing coverage and the amount of insurance required. If the borrower does not provide evidence of insurance within the response period stated in the notice, the servicer may obtain force-placed insurance and charge the borrower for its cost. Force-placed insurance protects the servicer's interest in the collateral; it does not replace the borrower's obligation to maintain coverage, and it may provide less protection than a policy the borrower would obtain independently.

When a borrower's policy lapses and the servicer has already placed coverage, the servicer must cancel that coverage and refund any related premium for the overlapping period once the borrower provides evidence of continuous coverage. Premium refunds are credited to the escrow account or applied to the outstanding balance, depending on the loan agreement.

A change in the annual premium affects the escrow analysis. An increase in the premium raises the projected disbursements and may create an escrow shortage; a decrease may create a surplus. The servicer updates the escrow analysis when it learns of a premium change.

## Property Taxes

The servicer monitors property tax obligations through a tax service that reports the status of each taxing authority. Taxes are paid from the escrow account before the delinquency date when funds are available, so the lien is not placed at risk by an unpaid assessment. Where a penalty or interest is assessed because a payment was late, the servicer may advance the amount and later seek reimbursement from the borrower when the late payment resulted from an escrow shortage that the borrower caused by failing to fund the account.

If the servicer learns that a tax has become delinquent, it may advance the amount necessary to cure the delinquency to protect the lien, and then add the advance to the borrower's obligation. A tax advance is separate from escrow collections: the servicer may recover it even when the escrow account cannot fund it, and it may require repayment immediately or spread the recovery over the remaining escrow schedule.

Where the property is subject to more than one taxing authority, the servicer tracks each authority separately, because due dates and assessment cycles differ. A reassessment may change the tax amount after the escrow analysis has been completed; the servicer re-analyzes the account when it receives notice of a material change.

## Construction Draw Administration

For construction loans, funds are disbursed in stages against approved draw requests rather than in a single advance. Each draw request states the work completed, the amount requested, and the cost breakdown by budget line. Before funding a draw, the servicer may require an inspection to confirm that the work described in the request has in fact been completed and that the remaining budget is sufficient to finish the project.

Retainage is the portion of each draw withheld until the associated work is inspected and accepted. The retainage percentage and the conditions for release are set in the loan agreement. Retainage protects the servicer against incomplete or defective work, and it is released only when the servicer confirms the work is complete to its satisfaction.

A draw is generally funded only when the borrower's contribution, if any, has been applied and when the project remains within the approved budget. If the cost to complete exceeds the remaining budget, the servicer may suspend further draws until the borrower funds the shortfall or the budget is amended. Suspending draws does not by itself make the loan delinquent, but an unfunded shortfall can delay completion and may lead to other remedies under the loan agreement.

Each disbursement is recorded against the appropriate budget line so the remaining budget can be tracked accurately. Interest typically accrues on the amounts actually disbursed rather than on the full commitment, so the timing of each draw affects the interest owed.

## Collateral and Lien Documentation

The servicer maintains the documentation that establishes and protects the lender's interest in the collateral, including the security instrument, any recorded amendments, and financing statements where personal property secures the obligation. A financing statement lapses unless a continuation statement is filed within the six months before its expiration; a lapsed financing statement can leave the lender's security interest unperfected, so the servicer monitors expiration dates and files continuations on time.

When the obligation is satisfied, the servicer releases the lien by recording a release or satisfaction and, where applicable, filing a termination of the financing statement. A release is issued only after all amounts owed have been received and any hold, such as a pending dispute, has been resolved.

A subordination agreement changes the priority of the lender's lien relative to another lien. The servicer may agree to subordinate the lien only when the loan agreement and applicable requirements permit it and when the lender's position remains adequately protected.

If collateral is damaged or destroyed, the servicer manages any insurance proceeds and applies them according to the loan agreement, which may require the proceeds to be used to restore the property or to reduce the outstanding balance.

## Servicing Transfer Procedures

When servicing rights are transferred from one servicer to another, the transferor and the transferee coordinate the transfer of records, funds, and borrower communications. The servicer provides the borrower with a notice of transfer that identifies the transferee, the transfer effective date, and the address and contact information for payments and inquiries after the transfer.

The notice must be provided within the period required by applicable rules and in a form the borrower can retain. During a defined period after the transfer, a payment mistakenly sent to the transferor must be forwarded to the transferee without assessing a penalty against the borrower, provided the payment was sent to the address and within the timeframe stated in the transfer notice.

On the transfer effective date, the transferee becomes responsible for escrow administration, tax and insurance monitoring, and all borrower communications. Records transferred include the payment history, the current escrow analysis, the tax and insurance status, and any pending loss mitigation or dispute matter. A pending matter does not restart when servicing transfers; it continues with the transferee from the point it had reached.

The servicer must reconcile the escrow balance and any suspense funds as part of the transfer so that borrower funds are accounted for accurately. Suspense funds, which are payments held but not yet applied, transfer with the loan and must be applied consistently with the borrower's original instructions.
